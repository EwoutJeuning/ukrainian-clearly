After this page you will know the Ukrainian personal pronouns: the words for *I*, *you*, *he*, *she*, *it*, *we* and *they*. You will know when to use <Uk>ти</Uk> and when to use <Uk>ви</Uk>, the two words for *you*. You will also be able to use the words for *my*, *your*, *his*, *her*, *our* and *their*, and make them agree with the noun.

## Personal pronouns

These are the pronouns in their dictionary form, the nominative. This is the form used for the subject of a sentence.

<ExampleList title="Pronouns as the subject">

  <Example uk="Він лі́кар." en="He is a doctor." />
  <Example uk="Вона́ вчи́телька." en="She is a teacher." />
  <Example uk="Ми вдо́ма." en="We are at home." />
  <Example uk="Вони́ тут." en="They are here." />
</ExampleList>

A few points to notice:

- **<Uk>Я</Uk> has no capital letter** inside a sentence, unlike English *I*.
- **<Uk>Він</Uk>, <Uk>вона́</Uk> and <Uk>воно́</Uk> follow grammatical gender**, not just sex. A table is <Uk>він</Uk> and a book is <Uk>вона́</Uk>, because <Uk>стіл</Uk> is masculine and <Uk>кни́га</Uk> is feminine. [Nouns and gender](/grammar/nouns-and-gender/) explains this.
- **<Uk>Вони́</Uk> is used for any group**, whatever the gender of the people or things in it.
- **The pronoun is often left out** when the verb ending already shows the person: <Uk>Чита́ю.</Uk> means "I am reading". [Sentence structure](/grammar/sentence-structure/) has more on this.

## Two words for "you"

Ukrainian has two words for *you*, and the choice says something about your relationship with the person. This is roughly like *tu* and *vous* in French or *du* and *Sie* in German, though the habits are not exactly the same.

- **<Uk>Ти</Uk>** is for one person you are close to: a friend, a family member, a child. Young people often use it with each other from the start. Said to a stranger or to an older person you don't know well, it can sound rude or overfamiliar.
- **<Uk>Ви</Uk>** is for two or more people, whoever they are. It is also the polite way to speak to one person: someone you have just met, someone older, someone in a shop or an office.

People who start with <Uk>ви</Uk> often move to <Uk>ти</Uk> when they get to know each other, usually when one of them suggests it. If you are not sure, use <Uk>ви</Uk>.

<ExampleList title="Asking a name">
  <Example uk="Як тебе́ зва́ти?" en="What's your name?" note="Informal: ти. тебе́ is a case form of ти." />
  <Example uk="Як вас зва́ти?" en="What's your name?" note="Formal or plural: ви. вас is a case form of ви." />
</ExampleList>

<Uk>Ви</Uk> always takes the plural verb ending, even when it means one person. The verb form is the same as when you speak to a group.

<ExampleList title="The verb after the two words for “you”">
  <Example uk="Ти зна́єш?" en="Do you know?" note="Informal: ти." />
  <Example uk="Ви зна́єте?" en="Do you know?" note="Formal or plural: ви. The same form for one person or several." />
</ExampleList>

### A capital letter in letters and emails

In letters, emails and official documents, <Uk>Ви</Uk> and <Uk>Ваш</Uk> (your) are written with a capital letter when they are addressed politely to one specific person. The spelling rules ask for the capital only in texts like these, so this site writes <Uk>ви</Uk> with a small letter in dialogue and everyday examples.

<Example uk="Дя́кую Вам за лист." en="Thank you for your letter." note="Formal (one person, written): a capital in a letter or email." />

## Possessives: my, your, our, their

The words for *my*, *your* (informal), *our*, *your* (formal or plural) and *their* change their ending to agree with the noun that is owned. They agree in gender and number, just like adjectives. (They also change for case, which comes later.)

Choose the column by the gender of the noun that follows, not by the owner. A woman says <Uk>мій брат</Uk> (my brother), and a man says <Uk>моя́ сестра́</Uk> (my sister).

<ExampleList title="Agreement with the noun">
  <Example uk="Це мій брат." en="This is my brother." note="брат is masculine." />
  <Example uk="Це моя́ сестра́." en="This is my sister." note="сестра́ is feminine." />
  <Example uk="Це на́ше мі́сто." en="This is our town." note="мі́сто is neuter." />
  <Example uk="Це мої́ батьки́." en="These are my parents." note="батьки́ is plural." />
  <Example uk="Де твоя́ кни́га?" en="Where is your book?" note="Informal: ти, so твоя́." />
  <Example uk="Де ва́ша кварти́ра?" en="Where is your flat?" note="Formal or plural: ви, so ва́ша." />
  <Example uk="Це ї́хня ма́ма." en="This is their mum." />
</ExampleList>

As with <Uk>ти</Uk> and <Uk>ви</Uk>, <Uk>твій</Uk> is informal and <Uk>ваш</Uk> is formal or plural. Use the one that matches how you address the person.

## His and her

The words for *his* and *her* work differently. <Uk>Його́</Uk> (his, also *its*) and <Uk>її́</Uk> (her) never change. They depend only on the owner, as in English.

<ExampleList title="His and her">
  <Example uk="Це його́ брат." en="This is his brother." />
  <Example uk="Це його́ сестра́." en="This is his sister." note="його́ does not change for a feminine noun." />
  <Example uk="Це її́ брат." en="This is her brother." />
  <Example uk="Це її́ сестра́." en="This is her sister." />
</ExampleList>

So in Ukrainian, *my*, *your*, *our* and *their* agree with the thing owned, while *his* and *her* reflect the owner. English *his* and *her* also reflect the owner, so these two should feel familiar. (A rough comparison.)

### The right word for "their"

For *their*, use <Uk>ї́хній</Uk>, which agrees with the noun: <Uk>ї́хній брат</Uk>, <Uk>ї́хня ма́ма</Uk>. You will also hear <Uk>їх</Uk> used to mean *their*, as in <Uk>їх брат</Uk>. Ukrainian usage guides recommend <Uk>ї́хній</Uk>, and some call <Uk>їх</Uk> for *their* an error: <Uk>їх</Uk> normally means *them*.

## Other forms of the pronouns, briefly

Like nouns, pronouns have [cases](/grammar/case-overview/). You have already seen <Uk>тебе́</Uk> and <Uk>вас</Uk> in <Uk>Як тебе́ зва́ти?</Uk> and <Uk>Як вас зва́ти?</Uk> Some forms look very different from the dictionary form, so it is worth knowing that they exist.

After a preposition, the forms of <Uk>він</Uk>, <Uk>вона́</Uk>, <Uk>воно́</Uk> and <Uk>вони́</Uk> begin with <Uk>н-</Uk>. For example, one common way to say "I have" is "at me is": <Uk>у ме́не є</Uk>. (Ukrainian also says <Uk>я ма́ю</Uk>, from <Uk>ма́ти</Uk>, to have, and usage guides recommend it.) "He has" is <Uk>у ньо́го є</Uk>: <Uk>ньо́го</Uk> is the form of <Uk>він</Uk> used here.

<ExampleList title="Forms after a preposition">
  <Example uk="У ме́не є брат." en="I have a brother." note="Literally: at me there is a brother." />
  <Example uk="У ньо́го є сестра́." en="He has a sister." note="ньо́го is a form of він used after a preposition." />
</ExampleList>

The full set of pronoun forms comes on a later page. For now, learn these phrases as wholes.

## Common mistakes and limits

<Note type="mistake">
  **Making the possessive agree with the owner.** A woman talking about her brother says <Uk>мій брат</Uk>, not <Uk>моя́ брат</Uk>. The possessive follows the noun. Only <Uk>його́</Uk> and <Uk>її́</Uk> follow the owner.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Using <Uk>ти</Uk> with strangers.** It can sound rude. Start with <Uk>ви</Uk>, and switch to <Uk>ти</Uk> when the other person suggests it or clearly uses it with you.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Using <Uk>їх</Uk> for "their".** Say <Uk>ї́хня кварти́ра</Uk> (their flat), not <Uk>їх кварти́ра</Uk>.
</Note>

<Note type="remember">
  Ukrainian also has <Uk>свій</Uk>, meaning *one's own*. It is very common and works differently from the possessives on this page, so it has its own explanation later.
</Note>
