After this page you will know what a case is and why Ukrainian nouns change their endings. You will be able to name the seven cases and give one main job for each. You will also recognise a few case forms of one noun when you meet them in a sentence.

This page is for recognising cases, not for learning endings by heart. The full tables of endings come later, one step at a time.

## What a case is

A **case** is the form a noun takes for a particular job in the sentence. Ukrainian shows that job mainly with the ending of the noun. The dictionary form is one case, and the other forms have different endings.

English has a small remnant of this. Compare *he* and *him*: *He sees her* but *She sees him*. The word changes because its job changes. English does this only with a few pronouns, such as *he/him*, *she/her* and *who/whom*. Ukrainian does it with every noun. (This is a rough comparison, not an exact match.)

<ExampleList title="Same noun, different job, different ending">
  <Example uk="Це сестра́." en="This is my sister." note="сестра́ is the dictionary form: the subject." />
  <Example uk="Я ба́чу сестру́." en="I see my sister." note="сестру́ is the form for the direct object." />
</ExampleList>

Ukrainian has no words for *a* or *the*, and it often leaves out *my* when the meaning is clear. So <Uk>сестра́</Uk> can be translated "a sister", "the sister" or "my sister" depending on the context.

## Why the endings change

English mostly uses word order and prepositions to show who does what. Ukrainian uses endings instead, and often a preposition together with an ending.

- **Endings free up the word order.** Because the ending shows who is the subject and who is the object, the words can move around to change the emphasis. [Sentence structure](/grammar/sentence-structure/) shows how this works.
- **Endings do the work of some English prepositions.** English says *with a pen* and *to my sister*. Ukrainian can say the same with an ending alone: <Uk>ру́чкою</Uk> (with a pen), <Uk>сестрі́</Uk> (to my sister).
- **Some prepositions need a particular case.** After <Uk>без</Uk> (without) the noun takes the genitive. After <Uk>з</Uk> (with, together with) it takes the instrumental. So each time you learn a preposition, it helps to learn which case comes after it.

## The seven cases

Ukrainian nouns have seven cases. Ukrainian dictionaries and textbooks list them in a fixed order, the one used below. Each case has several uses. The job given here is only the main one, simplified, to help you recognise it.

The examples all use <Uk>сестра́</Uk> (sister), except the locative, which is easier to show with a place name.

### Nominative: the subject

The **nominative** is the dictionary form. It is the case of the subject, the one doing the action.

<Example uk="Це моя́ сестра́." en="This is my sister." />

### Genitive: "of", and "no …"

The **genitive** often means *of* or shows who something belongs to. It is also used after a negative such as "there is no…", and after some prepositions, such as <Uk>без</Uk> (without).

<ExampleList title="Genitive">
  <Example uk="Це кни́га сестри́." en="This is my sister's book." note="Literally: this is the book of my sister." />
  <Example uk="У ме́не нема́є сестри́." en="I don't have a sister." note="Learn this as a whole phrase for now." />
</ExampleList>

### Dative: "to" or "for" someone

The **dative** is used for the person something is given or said to, or done for. It also follows some verbs, such as <Uk>телефонува́ти</Uk> (to phone).

<Example uk="Я телефону́ю сестрі́." en="I'm phoning my sister." note="English needs no preposition here, but the idea is “making a call to my sister”." />

### Accusative: the direct object

The **accusative** is the case of the direct object: the person or thing the action is done to.

<Example uk="Я ба́чу сестру́." en="I see my sister." />

### Instrumental: "with" or "by means of"

The **instrumental** shows the tool or means used to do something. With the preposition <Uk>з</Uk> (with), it also shows who you do something together with.

<ExampleList title="Instrumental">
  <Example uk="Я пишу́ ру́чкою." en="I write with a pen." note="No preposition: the ending alone means “with, using”." />
  <Example uk="Я розмовля́ю із сестро́ю." en="I'm talking with my sister." note="із is a form of з (with) used before some consonants, including с." />
</ExampleList>

### Locative: the place, after a preposition

The **locative** is used only after a preposition. It often describes a place: where someone or something is. It is also called the prepositional case.

<Example uk="Я живу́ в Ки́єві." en="I live in Kyiv." note="Ки́їв becomes Ки́єві after в (in)." />

### Vocative: calling someone

The **vocative** is the form used when you speak to someone directly and call them by name or by a word like *Mum*.

<ExampleList title="Vocative">
  <Example uk="Се́стро!" en="Sister!" note="The stress moves to the first syllable: сестра́, but се́стро." />
  <Example uk="Ма́мо, дя́кую!" en="Thank you, Mum!" note="ма́ма becomes ма́мо." />
</ExampleList>

The forms come from the dictionary paradigm of <Uk>сестра́</Uk> in <Uk>Горох</Uk>. Three things are worth noticing:

- **Two cases can share a form.** The dative and the locative are both <Uk>сестрі́</Uk>. The rest of the sentence, and the preposition in the locative, tell you which one it is.
- **Stress can tell forms apart.** <Uk>Сестри́</Uk> (of a sister, genitive singular) and <Uk>се́стри</Uk> (sisters, nominative plural) are spelled the same. Only the stress is different.
- **Stress can move.** It falls on the ending in most of these forms, but on the first syllable in the vocative <Uk>се́стро</Uk>.

## Endings depend on the kind of noun

Each case does not have just one ending. The ending depends on the noun's gender and on what its dictionary form ends in. Compare the accusative, the direct-object form, of three nouns:

<ExampleList title="The accusative of three nouns">
  <Example uk="Я ба́чу сестру́." en="I see my sister." note="сестра́ → сестру́" />
  <Example uk="Я ба́чу бра́та." en="I see my brother." note="брат → бра́та" />
  <Example uk="Я ба́чу стіл." en="I see the table." note="стіл stays стіл: here the accusative looks like the dictionary form." />
</ExampleList>

This is why the cases are introduced in stages. First you learn to recognise them. Later you learn the endings for each kind of noun, a group at a time. Pronouns and adjectives have cases too: [Pronouns](/grammar/pronouns/) shows a few pronoun forms.

You will see these names in Ukrainian dictionaries, such as the inflection tables on <Uk>Горох</Uk>. The word for "case" is <Uk>відмі́нок</Uk>.

## Common mistakes and limits

<Note type="mistake">
  **Trying to learn every table at once.** At this stage, aim to notice that an ending has changed and to guess the job from the context. The endings will stick as you meet the same words again and again.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Assuming the first noun is the subject.** The ending, not the position, tells you the job. In <Uk>Сестру́ ба́чить ма́ма</Uk> (it's Mum who sees my sister) the subject is <Uk>ма́ма</Uk>, at the end. The ending <Uk>-у</Uk> shows that <Uk>сестру́</Uk> is the object.
</Note>

<Note type="remember">
  Learn verbs and prepositions together with the case they take: <Uk>без сестри́</Uk>, <Uk>із сестро́ю</Uk>, <Uk>телефону́ю сестрі́</Uk>. Phrases like these are easier to remember than lists of endings.
</Note>

<Note>
  The "main jobs" on this page are simplifications. Every case has more uses, and several prepositions can take more than one case with a change of meaning. Those details come on later pages.
</Note>
