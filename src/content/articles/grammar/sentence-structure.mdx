After this page you will be able to read and build simple Ukrainian sentences. You will know the usual word order and why it can change. You will also know three things English has that Ukrainian usually does without: words for *a* and *the*, the verb *to be* in the present tense, and subject pronouns. Questions and negation are covered briefly at the end.

## The usual order: subject, verb, object

In a neutral sentence, Ukrainian often uses the same order as English: the subject, then the verb, then the object.

<Example en="Mum is reading a book.">
  <strong>Ма́ма</strong> чита́є <strong>кни́гу</strong>.
</Example>

The difference is that Ukrainian does not rely on the order to show who does what. The endings do that. <Uk>Ма́ма</Uk> is in the dictionary form, the case of the subject. <Uk>Кни́гу</Uk> has the ending of the direct object; the dictionary form is <Uk>кни́га</Uk>. The subject of a sentence is usually in the dictionary form (the nominative case).

## Word order is flexible

Because the endings carry the grammar, you can move words around and the basic meaning stays the same. What changes is the emphasis. As a rule of thumb, the start of the sentence is what is already known, and the end is the new or important information. In speech, the last element usually carries the logical emphasis.

<ExampleList title="Same words, different emphasis">
  <Example uk="Ма́ма чита́є кни́гу." en="Mum is reading a book." note="Neutral. The new information is what she is reading." />
  <Example uk="Кни́гу чита́є ма́ма." en="The book is being read by Mum. / It's Mum who is reading the book." note="The book is already known. The new information is who is reading it." />
</ExampleList>

In the second sentence <Uk>кни́гу</Uk> comes first, but its ending still marks it as the object. A learner who reads only the word order would get the sentence backwards. So check the ending of the first noun before deciding it is the subject.

Some small words stay in front of the word they belong to and move with it. These include prepositions, conjunctions and the negative word <Uk>не</Uk>.

## There are no articles

Ukrainian has no words for *a*, *an* or *the*. <Uk>Кни́га</Uk> can mean "a book" or "the book", and the context decides. Word order does part of the job that articles do in English. In the examples above, the book at the end of the first sentence is new, so English says *a book*. At the start of the second sentence it is already known, so English says *the book*.

## No "to be" in the present tense

In the present tense, Ukrainian usually leaves out *am*, *is* and *are*. The two parts of the sentence simply stand side by side.

<ExampleList title="Sentences without a present-tense verb">
  <Example uk="Це кни́га." en="This is a book." />
  <Example uk="Це ка́ва." en="This is coffee." />
  <Example uk="Я студе́нт." en="I am a student." note="Said by a man." />
  <Example uk="Я студе́нтка." en="I am a student." note="Said by a woman: the feminine noun is студе́нтка." />
  <Example uk="Ма́ма вдо́ма." en="Mum is at home." />
</ExampleList>

When both parts are nouns in the dictionary form, the written language usually puts a dash between them. The dash marks the place where English has *is* or *are*. After <Uk>це</Uk>, as in <Uk>Це кни́га</Uk>, no dash is used.

<ExampleList title="With a dash">
  <Example uk="Ки́їв — столи́ця Украї́ни." en="Kyiv is the capital of Ukraine." />
  <Example uk="Мій брат — лі́кар, а сестра́ — учи́телька." en="My brother is a doctor, and my sister is a teacher." />
</ExampleList>

The verb *to be* is <Uk>бу́ти</Uk>. Its present-tense form <Uk>є</Uk> exists, but in sentences like these it is normally left out. In the past tense the verb is used, and it agrees with the subject:

<Example uk="Ма́ма була́ вдо́ма." en="Mum was at home." />

## Leaving out the subject pronoun

Ukrainian verbs have a different ending for each person in the present tense. The ending already shows who is doing the action, so the subject pronoun (*I*, *you*, *we* and so on) is often left out when the context makes it clear.

<ExampleList title="With and without the pronoun">
  <Example uk="Я чита́ю." en="I am reading." />
  <Example uk="Чита́ю." en="I am reading." note="The ending -ю already shows the person: I." />
  <Example en="“What are you doing?” “Reading.”" note="Informal: ти.">
    – Що ти ро́биш? – Чита́ю.
  </Example>
  <Example en="“Where do you live?” “I live in Kyiv.”" note="Formal (one person): ви, answered by one person.">
    – Де ви живете́? – Живу́ в Ки́єві.
  </Example>
</ExampleList>

The pronoun is kept when you need it for clarity or contrast. It also matters in the past tense. Past-tense forms change for gender and number, not for person: <Uk>чита́в</Uk> (masculine), <Uk>чита́ла</Uk> (feminine), <Uk>чита́ли</Uk> (plural). So <Uk>чита́в</Uk> on its own could mean "I read", "you read" or "he read", and the pronoun or the context tells you which.

## Questions, briefly

A yes/no question can have exactly the same words and order as a statement. Only the intonation changes: you say it as a question, stressing the word you are asking about. In writing, the question mark shows it.

<ExampleList title="Statement and question">
  <Example uk="Ти студе́нт." en="You are a student." note="Informal: ти." />
  <Example uk="Ти студе́нт?" en="Are you a student?" note="Informal: ти. Same words; only the intonation changes." />
  <Example uk="Це ка́ва?" en="Is this coffee?" />
</ExampleList>

A question with a question word puts that word at the front, and the question word carries the emphasis.

<ExampleList title="Question words at the front">
  <Example uk="Що це?" en="What is this?" />
  <Example uk="Де ма́ма?" en="Where is Mum?" />
</ExampleList>

## Negation, briefly

To make a sentence negative, put <Uk>не</Uk> directly in front of the word you are negating. In a sentence without a verb, it goes in front of the noun.

<ExampleList title="Negation with не">
  <Example uk="Я не зна́ю." en="I don't know." />
  <Example uk="Я не студе́нт." en="I'm not a student." note="Said by a man." />
  <Example uk="Це не ка́ва, а чай." en="This isn't coffee, it's tea." />
</ExampleList>

Ukrainian has no helper verb like English *do* in *I don't know*. <Uk>Не</Uk> goes straight before <Uk>зна́ю</Uk>.

## Summary

<GrammarTable caption="What English needs and Ukrainian usually does without">

| English | Ukrainian | Example |
| ------- | --------- | ------- |
| *a*, *an*, *the* | No articles | <Uk>Це кни́га.</Uk> This is a book. |
| *am*, *is*, *are* | Usually left out in the present tense | <Uk>Я студе́нт.</Uk> I am a student. |
| Subject pronoun | Often left out; the verb ending shows the person | <Uk>Чита́ю.</Uk> I am reading. |
| Fixed word order | Flexible; the order shows emphasis | <Uk>Кни́гу чита́є ма́ма.</Uk> It's Mum who is reading the book. |
| Question word order (*Are you…?*) | Same order as a statement, with question intonation | <Uk>Ти студе́нт?</Uk> Are you a student? (informal) |
| *do not* | <Uk>не</Uk> before the negated word | <Uk>Я не зна́ю.</Uk> I don't know. |

</GrammarTable>

## Common mistakes and limits

<Note type="mistake">
  **Adding a word for "is".** <Uk>Я студе́нт</Uk> is a complete sentence. Do not add <Uk>є</Uk> to every sentence. Usage guides list <Uk>явля́тися</Uk> in the sense "to be" as an error to avoid. Use <Uk>бу́ти</Uk>, or simply leave the verb out.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Taking the first noun as the subject.** In <Uk>Кни́гу чита́є ма́ма</Uk> the subject is at the end. Look at the endings, not only the order.
</Note>

<Note type="remember">
  **Flexible does not mean random.** Every order is grammatical, but each one puts the emphasis in a different place. While you are learning, the neutral subject–verb–object order is a safe default. Pay attention to the order you hear and read: the feel for it comes with practice.
</Note>

<Note>
  The dash between two nouns is a rule of written Ukrainian. No dash is used when the subject is a personal pronoun, as in <Uk>Я студе́нт</Uk>, or when <Uk>не</Uk> comes before the second part.
</Note>
