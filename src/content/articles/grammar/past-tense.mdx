After this page you will be able to form the past tense of most Ukrainian verbs from the infinitive. You will know why the past tense changes for gender and number but not for person, and where to put the stress. At the end there is a short first look at verb aspect.

## One past tense

Everyday Ukrainian uses one past tense. One form covers what English splits into several. As a rough comparison only: <Uk>чита́в</Uk> can correspond to English *read*, *was reading* or *has read*, and the context decides.

The good news is that the past tense has only four endings to learn, not six. The less familiar part is what those endings show: the **gender and number** of the subject, not the person.

## How to form it

Start from the infinitive, the dictionary form ending in <Uk>-ти</Uk>. Take off <Uk>-ти</Uk> to get the stem. Then add one of four endings:

- <Uk>-в</Uk> for a masculine singular subject;
- <Uk>-ла</Uk> for a feminine singular subject;
- <Uk>-ло</Uk> for a neuter singular subject;
- <Uk>-ли</Uk> for any plural subject.

This pattern covers most verbs whose infinitive has a vowel before <Uk>-ти</Uk>. Notice that you build the past tense from the infinitive, not from the present tense. <Uk>Писа́ти</Uk> has the present-tense form <Uk>пишу́</Uk> (I write), but its past tense is regular: <Uk>писа́в</Uk>, <Uk>писа́ла</Uk>.

## Agreement: gender and number, not person

In the present tense the verb ending shows the person: *I*, *you*, *he*, and so on. In the past tense it does not. Instead the verb agrees with the subject in gender (in the singular) and number, the way an adjective does.

<GrammarTable
  caption="чита́ти (to read), past tense"
  headers={['Subject', 'Ukrainian', 'English (rough)']}
  rows={[
    ['I (a man)', 'я чита́в', 'I read, I was reading'],
    ['I (a woman)', 'я чита́ла', 'I read, I was reading'],
    ['you, informal (to a man)', 'ти чита́в', 'you read'],
    ['you, informal (to a woman)', 'ти чита́ла', 'you read'],
    ['he / she / it', 'він чита́в / вона́ чита́ла / воно́ чита́ло', 'he / she / it read'],
    ['we', 'ми чита́ли', 'we read'],
    ['you (formal or plural)', 'ви чита́ли', 'you read'],
    ['they', 'вони́ чита́ли', 'they read'],
  ]}
  ukColumns={[1]}
/>

Three things follow from this.

- **With <Uk>я</Uk> and <Uk>ти</Uk>, the form depends on who is meant.** A man says <Uk>я чита́в</Uk>; a woman says <Uk>я чита́ла</Uk>. When you speak to someone as <Uk>ти</Uk>, choose the form for that person.
- **The plural has one form for all genders.** <Uk>Чита́ли</Uk> is used for *we*, *you* (plural) and *they*, whoever they are.
- **Formal <Uk>ви</Uk> always takes the plural form,** even when you are speaking politely to one person.

<ExampleList title="Past-tense agreement in sentences">
  <Example uk="Я вчо́ра працюва́в." en="I worked yesterday." note="Said by a man." />
  <Example uk="Я вчо́ра працюва́ла." en="I worked yesterday." note="Said by a woman." />
  <Example uk="Ти спав?" en="Were you sleeping?" note="Informal: ти, said to a man." />
  <Example uk="Ма́ма працюва́ла." en="Mum was working." />
  <Example uk="Кошеня́ спа́ло." en="The kitten was sleeping." note="кошеня́ is neuter, so the verb takes -ло." />
  <Example uk="Ми жили́ в Ки́єві." en="We lived in Kyiv." />
  <Example uk="Ви були́ вдо́ма?" en="Were you at home?" note="Formal or plural: ви. The form is plural even when it is said politely to one person." />
</ExampleList>

Because the form does not show the person, the subject pronoun is usually kept in the past tense. <Uk>Чита́в</Uk> on its own could mean *I read*, *you read* or *he read*. [Sentence structure](/grammar/sentence-structure/) explains when pronouns can be left out.

## Where the stress falls

### Usually where it is in the infinitive

For most verbs the stress stays on the same syllable as in the infinitive, in all four forms: <Uk>чита́ти</Uk> → <Uk>чита́в</Uk>, <Uk>чита́ла</Uk>, <Uk>чита́ли</Uk>; <Uk>говори́ти</Uk> → <Uk>говори́в</Uk>, <Uk>говори́ла</Uk>, <Uk>говори́ли</Uk>. All the verbs in the first table work like this.

### Some short verbs move the stress to the ending

In a few common short verbs, the stress moves to the ending in the feminine, neuter and plural forms.

<GrammarTable
  caption="Stress moving to the ending"
  headers={['Infinitive', 'Masculine', 'Feminine', 'Neuter', 'Plural']}
  rows={[
    ['бу́ти (to be)', 'був', 'була́', 'було́', 'були́'],
    ['жи́ти (to live)', 'жив', 'жила́', 'жило́', 'жили́'],
    ['да́ти (to give)', 'дав', 'дала́', 'дало́', 'дали́'],
  ]}
  ukColumns={[0, 1, 2, 3, 4]}
/>

This is not a rule you can apply to every short verb. <Uk>Спа́ти</Uk> (to sleep) looks similar, but keeps the stress on the stem: <Uk>спав</Uk>, <Uk>спа́ла</Uk>, <Uk>спа́ли</Uk>. When you learn a short verb, learn its feminine past form too, and check the stress in a dictionary.

## Verbs with a different masculine form

A smaller group of verbs has a stem that ends in a consonant, or an irregular stem. In the masculine they have no <Uk>-в</Uk>, and the vowel of the stem may change. The other forms add <Uk>-ла</Uk>, <Uk>-ло</Uk> and <Uk>-ли</Uk> as usual. In the three verbs below the stress is on the ending, but this is not true of every such verb.

<GrammarTable
  caption="Some common verbs with a different masculine form"

    ['іти́ (to go, on foot)', 'ішо́в', 'ішла́', 'ішли́'],
  ]}
  ukColumns={[0, 1, 2, 3]}
/>

<Uk>Іти́</Uk> also has the form <Uk>йти</Uk>, with the past forms <Uk>йшов</Uk>, <Uk>йшла</Uk>, <Uk>йшли</Uk>. Treat these verbs as items to learn one by one.

## Reflexive verbs

Verbs with <Uk>-ся</Uk> at the end (reflexive verbs) form the past tense in the same way and keep <Uk>-ся</Uk> at the very end: <Uk>вчи́тися</Uk> (to study, to learn) → <Uk>вчи́вся</Uk>, <Uk>вчи́лася</Uk>, <Uk>вчи́лося</Uk>, <Uk>вчи́лися</Uk>.

<ExampleList title="A reflexive verb">
  <Example uk="Та́то вчи́вся в Ки́єві." en="Dad studied in Kyiv." />
  <Example uk="Сестра́ вчи́лася в Ки́єві." en="My sister studied in Kyiv." />
</ExampleList>

## A first look at aspect

Most Ukrainian verbs come in pairs. One verb of the pair, the **imperfective**, presents an action as a process, or as something that happened repeatedly. The other, the **perfective**, presents it as completed, with a result. The perfective is often the same verb with a prefix: <Uk>чита́ти</Uk> and <Uk>прочита́ти</Uk> both mean "to read". This difference is called **aspect**.

Both verbs of a pair form the past tense in exactly the same way. The difference is in the meaning.

<ExampleList title="Imperfective and perfective in the past">
  <Example uk="Я вчо́ра чита́в кни́гу." en="I was reading a book yesterday. / I read a book yesterday." note="Imperfective: the focus is on the activity. Said by a man." />
  <Example uk="Я прочита́в кни́гу." en="I have read the book. / I finished the book." note="Perfective: the reading is complete. Said by a man." />
  <Example uk="Я прочита́ла кни́гу." en="I have read the book. / I finished the book." note="Perfective, said by a woman." />
</ExampleList>

For now, it is enough to recognise that two verbs can share one English translation. The <Uk>Горох</Uk> dictionary marks each verb as imperfective (<Uk>недоко́наний вид</Uk>) or perfective (<Uk>доко́наний вид</Uk>). Aspect gets its own page later.

## Common mistakes and limits

<Note type="mistake">
  **Choosing the form by person.** There is no special past form for *I* or *you*. Choose it by gender and number. A man says <Uk>я чита́в</Uk> and a woman says <Uk>я чита́ла</Uk>; the same goes for the person you call <Uk>ти</Uk>.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Singular forms with formal <Uk>ви</Uk>.** Even when <Uk>ви</Uk> means one person, the verb is plural: <Uk>Ви були́ вдо́ма?</Uk>
</Note>

<Note type="remember">
  The pattern infinitive minus <Uk>-ти</Uk>, plus <Uk>-в</Uk>, <Uk>-ла</Uk>, <Uk>-ло</Uk>, <Uk>-ли</Uk> covers most verbs. Short verbs and verbs with a consonant stem need checking, both for the form and for the stress.
</Note>
