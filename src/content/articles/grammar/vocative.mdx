After this page you will be able to call someone by name in Ukrainian: <Uk>Оле́но!</Uk>, <Uk>Іва́не!</Uk>, <Uk>Андрі́ю!</Uk> You will know the main vocative endings for women's and men's names and for words like *Mum*, *son* and *friend*. You will also be able to address someone politely with a first name and patronymic, or with <Uk>па́не</Uk> and <Uk>па́ні</Uk>.

## What the vocative is for

The **[vocative](/glossary/#vocative)** is the form of a noun used when you speak *to* someone: when you call them, greet them, thank them or ask them something. The [case overview](/grammar/case-overview/) introduced it with <Uk>се́стро</Uk> and <Uk>ма́мо</Uk>.

In Ukrainian the vocative is the normal, expected way to address a person. The official spelling standard gives vocative forms for names, patronymics, family words and titles, and it puts both words of an address in the vocative: <Uk>па́не Іва́не</Uk>, not *<Uk>па́не Іва́н</Uk>*.

<ExampleList title="Addressing people">
  <Example uk="Приві́т, Оле́но!" en="Hi, Olena!" note="Оле́на → Оле́но." />
  <Example uk="Дя́кую, ма́мо!" en="Thank you, Mum!" note="ма́ма → ма́мо." />
  <Example uk="До́брий день, па́не Іва́не!" en="Good afternoon, Mr Ivan!" note="Both words change: пан → па́не, Іва́н → Іва́не." />
  <Example uk="Тара́се, ти вдо́ма?" en="Taras, are you at home?" note="Informal: ти. Тара́с → Тара́се." />
</ExampleList>

In writing, the form of address is separated from the rest of the sentence by commas, or followed by an exclamation mark. Greetings are covered in [Greetings and goodbyes](/sentences/greetings/).

## Women's names and other feminine nouns

Feminine nouns ending in <Uk>-а</Uk> or <Uk>-я</Uk> take one of three endings in the vocative.

    ['-о', 'Оле́на', 'Оле́но', 'Olena'],
    ['-о', 'О́льга', 'О́льго', 'Olha'],
    ['-о', 'Окса́на', 'Окса́но', 'Oksana'],
    ['-о', 'ма́ма', 'ма́мо', 'Mum'],
    ['-о', 'сестра́', 'се́стро', 'sister'],
    ['-е', 'На́стя', 'На́сте', 'Nastia'],
    ['-є', 'Марі́я', 'Марі́є', 'Mariia'],
    ['-є', 'Ната́лія', 'Ната́ліє', 'Nataliia'],
    ['-ю', 'до́ня', 'до́ню', 'daughter (affectionate)'],
    ['-ю', 'бабу́ся', 'бабу́сю', 'granny'],
    ['-ю', 'Ле́ся', 'Ле́сю', 'Lesia'],
  ]}
  ukColumns={[0, 1, 2]}
/>

The pattern, as the spelling standard describes it:

- **<Uk>-а</Uk> becomes <Uk>-о</Uk>.** This is the most common pattern: <Uk>Оле́но</Uk>, <Uk>Окса́но</Uk>, <Uk>Іри́но</Uk>.
- **<Uk>-а</Uk> after <Uk>ж</Uk>, <Uk>ч</Uk>, <Uk>ш</Uk> or <Uk>щ</Uk> becomes <Uk>-е</Uk>:** <Uk>душа́</Uk> → <Uk>ду́ше</Uk> (soul, used to someone dear) [src: pravopys-2026, §74].
- **<Uk>-я</Uk> after a consonant becomes <Uk>-е</Uk>:** <Uk>На́сте</Uk>. After a vowel, as in <Uk>-ія</Uk>, it becomes <Uk>-є</Uk>: <Uk>Марі́є</Uk>, <Uk>Ната́ліє</Uk>.
- **Some affectionate words in <Uk>-я</Uk> take <Uk>-ю</Uk>:** <Uk>до́ню</Uk>, <Uk>бабу́сю</Uk>, <Uk>Ле́сю</Uk>. Learn these one by one.

The stress usually stays where it is in the dictionary form, but not always. <Uk>Сестра́</Uk> becomes <Uk>се́стро</Uk>, with the stress on the first syllable.

## Men's names and other masculine nouns

Masculine nouns that end in a consonant take <Uk>-е</Uk>, <Uk>-у</Uk> or <Uk>-ю</Uk>.

  rows={[
    ['-е', 'Іва́н', 'Іва́не', 'Ivan'],
    ['-е', 'Тара́с', 'Тара́се', 'Taras'],
    ['-е', 'Олекса́ндр', 'Олекса́ндре', 'Oleksandr'],
    ['-е', 'брат', 'бра́те', 'brother'],
    ['-е', 'пан', 'па́не', 'sir, Mr'],
    ['-е', 'друг', 'дру́же', 'friend'],
    ['-у', 'син', 'си́ну', 'son'],
    ['-у', 'ба́тько', 'ба́тьку', 'father'],
    ['-у', 'Оле́г', 'Оле́гу', 'Oleh'],
    ['-ю', 'Андрі́й', 'Андрі́ю', 'Andrii'],
    ['-ю', 'Сергі́й', 'Сергі́ю', 'Serhii'],
    ['-ю', 'Васи́ль', 'Васи́лю', 'Vasyl'],
    ['-ю', 'І́гор', 'І́горю', 'Ihor'],
    ['-ю', 'лі́кар', 'лі́карю', 'doctor'],
  ]}
  ukColumns={[0, 1, 2]}
/>

A rough guide to which ending to use:

- **<Uk>-е</Uk>** is the usual ending after a hard consonant: <Uk>Іва́не</Uk>, <Uk>Тара́се</Uk>, <Uk>бра́те</Uk>. Before it, <Uk>г</Uk> becomes <Uk>ж</Uk> and <Uk>к</Uk> becomes <Uk>ч</Uk>: <Uk>друг</Uk> → <Uk>дру́же</Uk>.
- **<Uk>-ю</Uk>** goes with soft nouns, including names in <Uk>-ій</Uk> and nouns in <Uk>-ь</Uk>: <Uk>Андрі́ю</Uk>, <Uk>Васи́лю</Uk>, and also <Uk>І́горю</Uk>, <Uk>лі́карю</Uk>, <Uk>учи́телю</Uk> (teacher).
- **<Uk>-у</Uk>** goes with a smaller group. It includes the family words <Uk>си́ну</Uk> and <Uk>ба́тьку</Uk>, nouns with suffixes such as <Uk>-ик</Uk> and <Uk>-ок</Uk>, and foreign names whose stem ends in <Uk>г</Uk>, <Uk>к</Uk> or <Uk>х</Uk>: <Uk>Джек</Uk> → <Uk>Дже́ку</Uk>.

Some nouns have two accepted forms. For <Uk>Оле́г</Uk> the spelling standard gives <Uk>Оле́же</Uk>, with <Uk>Оле́гу</Uk> as a variant [src: pravopys-2026, §87]; the dictionary lists <Uk>Оле́гу</Uk> first. Both are correct.

### Masculine names in -о and -а

Men's names that end in a vowel follow their ending, not their gender:

- **In <Uk>-о</Uk>**, they take <Uk>-е</Uk>, and the stress may move: <Uk>Петро́</Uk> → <Uk>Пе́тре</Uk>, <Uk>Дмитро́</Uk> → <Uk>Дми́тре</Uk>, <Uk>Миха́йло</Uk> → <Uk>Миха́йле</Uk>.
- **In <Uk>-а</Uk>**, they behave like <Uk>Оле́на</Uk> and take <Uk>-о</Uk>: <Uk>Мико́ла</Uk> → <Uk>Мико́ло</Uk>, <Uk>коле́га</Uk> (colleague) → <Uk>коле́го</Uk>.

The word for *dad*, <Uk>та́то</Uk>, has the vocative <Uk>та́ту</Uk> in the spelling standard [src: pravopys-2026, §87]. The dictionary also gives <Uk>та́то</Uk>, which you will hear too.

<ExampleList title="Calling men and boys">
  <Example uk="Андрі́ю, де ти?" en="Andrii, where are you?" note="Informal: ти." />
  <Example uk="Си́ну, іди́ сюди́!" en="Come here, son!" note="Informal: іди́ is the ти form of the command." />
  <Example uk="Дя́кую, дру́же!" en="Thanks, my friend!" note="дру́же is warm and friendly." />
  <Example uk="Пе́тре, ти гото́вий?" en="Petro, are you ready?" note="Informal: ти. The stress moves: Петро́, but Пе́тре." />
</ExampleList>

## First name and patronymic

The polite, official way to address an adult is the first name followed by the patronymic, the name formed from the father's first name. <Uk>Іва́н Микола́йович</Uk> is Ivan, son of Mykola. <Uk>Оле́на Петрі́вна</Uk> is Olena, daughter of Petro.

When you address someone this way, **both words** go into the vocative.

<GrammarTable
  caption="Name and patronymic in the vocative"

    ['Окса́на Олекса́ндрівна', 'Окса́но Олекса́ндрівно'],
    ['Іва́н Микола́йович', 'Іва́не Микола́йовичу'],
    ['Тара́с Іва́нович', 'Тара́се Іва́новичу'],
  ]}
  ukColumns={[0, 1]}
/>

Patronymics in <Uk>-івна</Uk> (<Uk>-ївна</Uk>) take <Uk>-о</Uk>, like <Uk>Оле́на</Uk>. Patronymics in <Uk>-ович</Uk> (<Uk>-йович</Uk>) take <Uk>-у</Uk>.

<ExampleList title="Name and patronymic">
  <Example uk="Оле́но Петрі́вно, мо́жна запита́ти?" en="Olena Petrivna, may I ask something?" note="Polite: a first name and patronymic, for example to a teacher." />
  <Example uk="Іва́не Микола́йовичу, дя́кую вам!" en="Thank you, Ivan Mykolaiovych!" note="Formal (one person): вам is a form of ви." />
</ExampleList>

## Polite address: пане and пані

<Uk>Пан</Uk> (Mr, sir) and <Uk>па́ні</Uk> (Mrs, Ms, madam) are used before a first name or on their own.

- **<Uk>Пан</Uk> changes** like any masculine noun: <Uk>па́не</Uk>. The name after it changes too: <Uk>па́не Іва́не</Uk>, <Uk>па́не Андрі́ю</Uk>.
- **<Uk>Па́ні</Uk> never changes.** It has the same form in every case. Only the name after it changes: <Uk>па́ні Оле́но</Uk>, <Uk>па́ні Марі́є</Uk>.

<ExampleList title="With пане and пані">
  <Example uk="Па́не Андрі́ю, ви ма́єте хвили́нку?" en="Mr Andrii, do you have a minute?" note="Formal (one person): ви." />
  <Example uk="Па́ні Оле́но, до́брий ве́чір!" en="Good evening, Mrs Olena!" note="па́ні stays the same; Оле́на → Оле́но." />
  <Example uk="Шано́вна па́ні Окса́но!" en="Dear Mrs Oksana!" note="A common opening for a formal letter or email." />
</ExampleList>

With a surname, the rules allow more variation, and the surname may keep its dictionary form. This page sticks to first names.

## Adjectives in an address

An adjective that goes with the noun in an address does not take a special ending. It keeps the same form as in the nominative: <Uk>дороги́й дру́же</Uk> (dear friend), <Uk>шано́вний па́не</Uk> (dear sir), <Uk>шано́вна па́ні</Uk> (dear madam). Only the noun is in the vocative. [Adjectives and agreement](/grammar/adjectives-and-agreement/) has these nominative endings.

## The plural

In the plural, the vocative is the same as the nominative plural. There is nothing new to learn: <Uk>дру́зі</Uk> (friends) is both "friends" and "Friends!".

<ExampleList title="Addressing several people">
  <Example uk="Дру́зі, дя́кую!" en="Thank you, friends!" />
  <Example uk="Шано́вні па́ні та пано́ве!" en="Ladies and gentlemen!" note="пано́ве is a special plural form used in formal, ceremonial address." />
</ExampleList>

<Uk>Пано́ве</Uk> belongs to a small group of plural forms used in poetic and ceremonial speech. The ordinary plural of <Uk>пан</Uk> is <Uk>пани́</Uk>.

## Common mistakes and limits

<Note type="mistake">
  **Using the dictionary form to call someone.** In standard Ukrainian, say <Uk>Оле́но, приві́т!</Uk> and <Uk>Іва́не, дя́кую!</Uk>, not *<Uk>Оле́на, приві́т!</Uk>* The vocative is what people expect.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Changing only one word.** In <Uk>Оле́но Петрі́вно</Uk> and <Uk>па́не Іва́не</Uk>, both words are in the vocative. Avoid *<Uk>Оле́но Петрі́вна</Uk>* and *<Uk>па́не Іва́н</Uk>*.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Changing <Uk>па́ні</Uk>.** It has one form for every case: <Uk>па́ні Окса́но</Uk>, not *<Uk>па́не Окса́но</Uk>*. <Uk>Па́не</Uk> is only for men.
</Note>

<Note type="remember">
  The endings to remember first: <Uk>-а</Uk> → <Uk>-о</Uk> for most women's names, <Uk>-е</Uk> for most men's names that end in a consonant, and <Uk>-ю</Uk> for names in <Uk>-ій</Uk>. When you learn a new name, check its vocative in a dictionary. <Uk>Горох</Uk> shows it as the <Uk>кли́чний</Uk> row of the inflection table.
</Note>

<Note>
  Nouns like <Uk>ніч</Uk> (night) also have vocative forms, such as <Uk>но́че</Uk>, but the spelling standard notes that they are used mainly in poetry. This page covers the words you need to address people.
</Note>
