After this page you will know every possessive word in Ukrainian, how each one changes, and which ones do not change at all. You will be able to say *their* correctly with <Uk>ї́хній</Uk>, and you will know when to use <Uk>свій</Uk> (one's own): the word that keeps *He took his coat* from meaning someone else's coat.

[Pronouns](/grammar/pronouns/) introduced the possessives in the nominative, and [Adjectives in all cases](/grammar/adjective-cases/) gave the full tables for <Uk>мій</Uk> and <Uk>наш</Uk>. This page brings all of them together and adds <Uk>свій</Uk>.

So there are two kinds:

- **Possessives that agree with the noun**, like adjectives: <Uk>мій</Uk>, <Uk>твій</Uk>, <Uk>наш</Uk>, <Uk>ваш</Uk>, <Uk>ї́хній</Uk> and <Uk>свій</Uk>. They change for gender, number and case.
- **Possessives that never change:** <Uk>його́</Uk> and <Uk>її́</Uk>. They are the genitive forms of <Uk>він</Uk> and <Uk>вона́</Uk>, and depend only on the owner.

## The agreeing possessives in all cases

The spelling standard groups them by pattern [src: pravopys-2026, §110]:

- <Uk>мій</Uk>, <Uk>твій</Uk> and <Uk>свій</Uk> share one set of endings. The full table of <Uk>мій</Uk> is on [Adjectives in all cases](/grammar/adjective-cases/#my-your-our-possessives-in-all-cases);
- <Uk>наш</Uk> and <Uk>ваш</Uk> change like hard adjectives such as <Uk>нови́й</Uk>. The full table of <Uk>наш</Uk> is on the same page;
- <Uk>ї́хній</Uk> changes like soft adjectives such as <Uk>си́ній</Uk>. Its table is below.

In the masculine and neuter locative, the forms in <Uk>-ому</Uk>/<Uk>-єму</Uk> (<Uk>у моє́му</Uk>, <Uk>у на́шому</Uk>) and the shorter forms (<Uk>у мої́м</Uk>, <Uk>у на́шім</Uk>) are both correct. A usage guide notes that the longer forms are more common, and the only ones used in scientific and official writing [src: goroh-usage].

## його and її: his and her

<Uk>Його́</Uk> (his, its) and <Uk>її́</Uk> (her) never change, whatever the noun after them: <Uk>його́ брат</Uk>, <Uk>його́ сестра́</Uk>, <Uk>з його́ сестро́ю</Uk>, <Uk>для її́ діте́й</Uk>.

Two details:

- **No <Uk>н-</Uk>.** After a preposition the *personal* pronouns take <Uk>н-</Uk> (<Uk>до ньо́го</Uk>, *to him*), but the possessives do not: <Uk>до його́ бра́та</Uk> (to his brother). [Personal pronouns in all cases](/grammar/pronoun-cases/) explains this.
- **No stress shift.** When the preposition belongs to the noun, not to the possessive, the stress of <Uk>його́</Uk> and <Uk>її́</Uk> stays where it is: <Uk>до його́ ха́ти</Uk>, <Uk>до її́ до́му</Uk> [src: pravopys-2026, §113, note 2].

<ExampleList title="його́ and її́">
  <Example uk="Я зна́ю його́ сестру́." en="I know his sister." />
  <Example uk="Ми говори́ли з її́ батька́ми." en="We talked to her parents." />
  <Example uk="Я йду до його́ бра́та." en="I'm going to his brother's." note="його́ means his: no н-." />
</ExampleList>

## Their: їхній, not їх

<Uk>Їх</Uk> is the genitive and accusative of <Uk>вони́</Uk>: *them*. Usage guides recommend <Uk>ї́хній</Uk> for *their*, and some call <Uk>їх</Uk> in this meaning an error [src: goroh-usage]. A simple test from one guide: if you can ask <Uk>чий?</Uk> (whose?) about the phrase, use <Uk>ї́хній</Uk> in the right form [src: goroh-usage].

<ExampleList title="ї́хній or їх?">
  <Example uk="Це ї́хні кни́ги." en="These are their books." note="Whose books? ї́хні." />
  <Example uk="Я відпові́в на ї́хні пита́ння." en="I answered their questions." note="Said by a man. Whose questions? ї́хні." />
  <Example uk="Я їх зна́ю." en="I know them." note="Not a possessive: їх means them." />
</ExampleList>

## Ваш with a capital letter

In letters, emails and official documents, <Uk>Ви</Uk> and <Uk>Ваш</Uk> are written with a capital letter when addressed politely to one person [src: pravopys-2026, §60].

<Example uk="Дя́кую за Ваш лист." en="Thank you for your letter." note="Formal (one person, written)." />

## свій: one's own

<Uk>Свій</Uk> means *one's own*: something that belongs to the person the sentence is about [src: goroh-dictionary]. In practice this is the subject of the verb, the person who does the action. It changes exactly like <Uk>мій</Uk> [src: pravopys-2026, §110]: <Uk>свій</Uk>, <Uk>своя́</Uk>, <Uk>своє́</Uk>, <Uk>свої́</Uk>; <Uk>свого́</Uk>, <Uk>своє́ї</Uk>, <Uk>свої́м</Uk>, <Uk>своє́ю</Uk> and so on.

<Uk>Свій</Uk> works like the reflexive pronoun <Uk>себе́</Uk> on [Personal pronouns in all cases](/grammar/pronoun-cases/): it points back to the subject, whoever that is. So one word covers *my own*, *your own*, *his own*, *her own*, *our own* and *their own*.

### With he, she and they: свій changes the meaning

With a subject in the third person (<Uk>він</Uk>, <Uk>вона́</Uk>, <Uk>воно́</Uk>, <Uk>вони́</Uk>, or a noun), the choice matters. <Uk>Свій</Uk> means the subject's own thing. <Uk>Його́</Uk>, <Uk>її́</Uk> and <Uk>ї́хній</Uk> point to **someone else** who has already been mentioned.

<ExampleList title="свій or його́?">
  <Example uk="Андрі́й узя́в свою́ ку́ртку." en="Andrii took his (own) jacket." note="свою́: Andrii's jacket." />
  <Example uk="Андрі́й узя́в його́ ку́ртку." en="Andrii took his jacket." note="його́: another man's jacket, someone mentioned before." />
  <Example uk="Оле́на розмовля́є зі своє́ю ма́мою." en="Olena is talking to her (own) mother." note="Olena's mother." />
  <Example uk="Оле́на розмовля́є з її́ ма́мою." en="Olena is talking to her mother." note="Another woman's mother." />
  <Example uk="Студе́нти принесли́ свої́ кни́ги." en="The students brought their (own) books." />
</ExampleList>

English *his* and *her* are ambiguous here. Ukrainian is not, so think about whose thing it is before you choose.

### With I, you and we: both are found

With <Uk>я</Uk>, <Uk>ти</Uk>, <Uk>ми</Uk> and <Uk>ви</Uk>, there is no ambiguity: <Uk>мій</Uk> and <Uk>свій</Uk> both point to the speaker. Both are used, and the dictionary has literary examples of <Uk>свій</Uk> with <Uk>я</Uk>, such as <Uk>Свій літерату́рний смак я ви́робив…</Uk> (I developed my literary taste…, Kotsiubynsky) [src: goroh-dictionary].

<ExampleList title="With я, ти, ми and ви">
  <Example uk="Я люблю́ своє́ мі́сто." en="I love my town." note="свій: my own." />
  <Example uk="Я люблю́ моє́ мі́сто." en="I love my town." note="Also correct." />
  <Example uk="Візьми́ свою́ парасо́льку." en="Take your umbrella." note="Informal: візьми́ is the ти form of the command. The person told to act is the subject, so свою́ means yours." />
  <Example uk="Ви не забу́ли свої́ ключі́?" en="You haven't forgotten your keys?" note="Formal or plural: ви." />
</ExampleList>

### When the owner is not the subject

Sometimes the owner is in another case, for example in the phrase <Uk>у ко́жного</Uk> (each person has). <Uk>Свій</Uk> can still refer to that person, as in the saying recommended by one usage guide: <Uk>У ко́жного свій смак.</Uk> (Everyone has their own taste.) [src: goroh-usage].

### Do not overuse свій

<Uk>Свій</Uk> is not needed when it is already obvious whose thing it is. A usage guide gives <Uk>Я веду́ свій щоде́нник уже́ кі́лька років</Uk> as an example where <Uk>свій</Uk> can simply be left out: <Uk>Я веду́ щоде́нник уже́ кі́лька років</Uk> (I've been keeping a diary for several years) [src: goroh-usage].

Usage guides also recommend some set phrases [src: goroh-usage]:

- <Uk>свого́ ча́су</Uk> (at one time, back then), rather than *<Uk>у свій час</Uk>*;
- <Uk>на мою́ ду́мку</Uk> (in my opinion), rather than *<Uk>за моє́ю ду́мкою</Uk>*.

## Common mistakes and limits

<Note type="mistake">
  **<Uk>його́</Uk> or <Uk>її́</Uk> for the subject's own thing.** <Uk>Він узя́в його́ ку́ртку</Uk> means someone else's jacket. For his own, say <Uk>Він узя́в свою́ ку́ртку</Uk>.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **<Uk>Їх</Uk> for *their*.** Say <Uk>ї́хня кварти́ра</Uk>, <Uk>у ї́хньому буди́нку</Uk>, not *<Uk>їх кварти́ра</Uk>* [src: goroh-usage].
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Colloquial forms such as <Uk>мойого</Uk>.** The literary forms are <Uk>мого́</Uk>, <Uk>твого́</Uk>, <Uk>свого́</Uk>, <Uk>моє́му</Uk>. A usage guide classes <Uk>мойого</Uk>, <Uk>твойого</Uk>, <Uk>свойого</Uk> and <Uk>мойому</Uk> as non-standard [src: goroh-usage].
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  **Making the possessive agree with the owner.** A woman says <Uk>мій брат</Uk>, and a man says <Uk>моя́ сестра́</Uk>. The agreeing possessives follow the noun, not the owner.
</Note>

<Note type="remember">
  Ask two questions: does the word agree with the noun (<Uk>мій</Uk>, <Uk>твій</Uk>, <Uk>наш</Uk>, <Uk>ваш</Uk>, <Uk>ї́хній</Uk>, <Uk>свій</Uk>) or never change (<Uk>його́</Uk>, <Uk>її́</Uk>)? And is the owner the subject? If so, <Uk>свій</Uk> is the safe choice. With *he*, *she* and *they*, it is what tells the listener that the thing is the subject’s own.
</Note>

<Note>
  The question word <Uk>чий?</Uk> (whose?) changes in a similar way: <Uk>чий</Uk>, <Uk>чия́</Uk>, <Uk>чиє́</Uk>, <Uk>чиї́</Uk> [src: pravopys-2026, §112]. Its full forms are not covered here.
</Note>
