*Ukrainian, Clearly* explains how Ukrainian works, in English, for complete beginners. Every page explains the patterns behind the examples, so that you can build sentences of your own rather than memorise phrases.

## What the site covers

The site teaches standard modern Ukrainian: the language of the current official spelling standard, <Uk>«Український правопис»</Uk>, in force since 28 March 2026. It is the same orthography as the 2019 edition, with editorial corrections only.

The material is for beginners. It covers the alphabet and pronunciation, the core of the grammar, sentence patterns for everyday situations, and small vocabulary lists. It does not cover regional dialects, and it does not teach mixed Ukrainian-Russian speech (surzhyk) or Russian forms as alternatives.

The grammar pages give beginner generalisations. Where a rule has exceptions, or a statement is a simplification, the page says so.

## How the articles are written and checked

The articles are drafted with the help of an AI model and then checked against the references listed below. They are not written by a named expert, and the site does not claim to be. The checks are:

- **Stress:** every stressed word in an example, table or list is checked in a dictionary that marks stress, usually <Uk>Горох</Uk> or <Uk>«Словники України»</Uk>. If the dictionaries disagree, or a word has two accepted stresses, it is left unmarked.
- **Spelling:** examples follow the current orthography, including the alternation of <Uk>у</Uk>/<Uk>в</Uk> and <Uk>з</Uk>/<Uk>із</Uk>/<Uk>зі</Uk>.
- **Claims:** grammar statements are checked against the dictionaries' definitions, the orthography, or the English-language sources below. Each page lists the sources it used.
- **Review:** each new batch of pages gets a separate accuracy review before it is published: a second pass over the stress, spelling, translations and claims.

Mistakes are still possible. A careful check is not the same as a native teacher's eye.

## Conventions on this site

### Stress marks

Ukrainian stress is free and can move, so the site marks it in examples with an accent over the stressed vowel: <Uk>мо́ва</Uk>, <Uk>приві́т</Uk>. Words of one syllable are not marked. Ukrainian is not normally written with stress marks; they are a learning aid. [Stress and transliteration](/alphabet/stress-and-transliteration/) explains this further.

### Transliteration

Where the site shows Ukrainian in Latin letters, it uses the official national system of Ukraine, approved in 2010. Transliteration is only a support: the Cyrillic always comes first, and the Latin form is not a guide to pronunciation.

### Pronunciation

Pronunciation is described in plain English on the alphabet pages, always as an approximation. English comparisons are checked against published phonetic descriptions of Ukrainian.

### Formal and informal "you"

Ukrainian has two words for *you*: <Uk>ти</Uk> (informal, one person) and <Uk>ви</Uk> (formal, or more than one person). Every example that uses one of them is labelled, so you know which to use. [Pronouns](/grammar/pronouns/) explains the difference.

### Translations

English translations are natural English, not word-for-word. Where the Ukrainian is built differently, a note gives the literal meaning.
