## What you will learn

This page covers two things that sit around the alphabet. **Stress**: Ukrainian stress can fall on any syllable, it can move, and ordinary text does not show it, so you need to learn it with each word. **Transliteration**: Ukraine has an official way of writing Ukrainian in Latin letters. It is useful for names, but it is not a pronunciation guide and it does not replace Cyrillic.

## Before you start

Read [The Ukrainian alphabet](/alphabet/) and [Pronunciation basics](/alphabet/pronunciation/) first. This page assumes you can read the letters and know what the vowels sound like.

## Stress can fall on any syllable

Every Ukrainian word of more than one syllable has one stressed syllable. Ukrainian stress is **free**: there is no fixed position such as "always the first syllable" or "always the second-to-last". The 2017 phonetic study shows that it can fall on any syllable of a word. Shevelov's encyclopedia entry adds that a stressed vowel is marked mainly by being longer, not by being much louder.

## How this site marks stress

Ukrainian books, newspapers, signs and websites normally **do not mark stress**. Dictionaries and learning materials do. On this site:

- The stressed vowel is marked with an acute accent placed over it: <Uk>мо́ва</Uk>, <Uk>вода́</Uk>. Technically this is the combining acute accent (Unicode U+0301), added after the vowel letter. The <Uk>Горох</Uk> dictionary uses the same character.
- Stress is marked in examples, tables and word lists. In English sentences a word may appear without the mark if it is marked in a nearby example.
- Words of one syllable are not marked, because there is only one vowel that could be stressed: <Uk>дім</Uk>, <Uk>п’ять</Uk>, <Uk>так</Uk>.
- Headings never carry stress marks.
- Every marked word on these pages has been checked in the <Uk>Горох</Uk> dictionary and, where it lists the word, in <Uk>«Словники України»</Uk> from the Ukrainian Lingua-Information Fund.

<Note type="remember">
  The stress mark is a learning aid, not part of the spelling. When you write Ukrainian yourself, leave it out, just as Ukrainians do.
</Note>

<Note type="mistake">
  Do not guess stress from English or from another language. A word such as <Uk>авто́бус</Uk> may not be stressed where you expect. When you learn a new word, check its stress in a dictionary that marks it.
</Note>

## The official transliteration

Transliteration means writing a language in another alphabet, letter by letter. Ukraine has an official system, approved by Cabinet of Ministers Resolution No. 55 of 27 January 2010 <Uk>«Про впорядкування транслітерації українського алфавіту латиницею»</Uk> ("On regulating the transliteration of the Ukrainian alphabet into Latin script"). It sets how surnames, first names and place names are spelled in Latin letters. In 2012 the United Nations recommended the same system for Ukrainian geographical names.

This site uses this system whenever it shows Latin letters, and applies it exactly.

The resolution adds three notes. The combination <Uk>зг</Uk> is written **zgh**, so that it is not confused with zh (<Uk>ж</Uk>). The soft sign and the apostrophe are **left out**. Names are transliterated letter by letter.

The stress marks are on the Cyrillic only. The official Latin spelling has no way to show stress, and this site does not add any.

## What transliteration cannot do

<Note type="mistake">
  **Transliteration is not a pronunciation guide.** It tells you how to spell a name in Latin letters, not how to say it. Read Kyiv-style spellings with care:

  - **h is not English h.** It stands for <Uk>г</Uk>, the voiced throat sound described in [Pronunciation basics](/alphabet/pronunciation/).
  - **y and i are easy to read the wrong way round.** In Kyiv (<Uk>Ки́їв</Uk>) the y stands for <Uk>и</Uk>; in Vinnytsia (<Uk>Ві́нниця</Uk>) the first i stands for <Uk>і</Uk> and the y for <Uk>и</Uk>.
  - **Information is lost.** The soft sign and the apostrophe disappear, so <Uk>Ма́р’їне</Uk> becomes Marine.
  - **It cannot be reversed reliably.** Several letters can give the same Latin result: <Uk>й</Uk>, the start of <Uk>є, ї, ю, я</Uk>, and <Uk>и</Uk> can all produce y, and both <Uk>й</Uk> and <Uk>ї</Uk> can produce i in the middle of a word. You cannot rebuild the Cyrillic from the Latin.
  - **Stress is not shown.**
</Note>

You will also see other ways of writing Ukrainian in Latin letters. The 2017 phonetic study, for example, uses a scholarly system and writes the capital as Kyjiv. Such systems give different results for some letters. This site does not mix them with the official system.

## Why Cyrillic stays first

On this site, Ukrainian is always shown in Cyrillic first. Transliteration, when it appears at all, is optional support for readers who are still learning the letters. It is never the only form of an example. This is a site policy, for three reasons:

- Ukrainian is written in Cyrillic. Everything you will read, from signs to books, uses it.
- The Latin form loses information that matters for reading and pronunciation: softness, the apostrophe, the difference between <Uk>й</Uk> and <Uk>и</Uk> in some positions, and stress.
- Every later page on this site depends on reading Cyrillic. Relying on Latin spelling makes it harder to read real Ukrainian.
