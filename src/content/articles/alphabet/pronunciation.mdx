## What you will learn

This page explains the sound patterns behind the letters: how the six vowels behave with and without stress, what "soft" consonants are, and how <Uk>Г, Ґ, В, Й, Щ</Uk> and the apostrophe are pronounced. It ends with the mistakes English speakers most often make.

All comparisons with English are **approximate**. The descriptions of Ukrainian sounds follow two English-language references: a peer-reviewed phonetic description of Ukrainian from the *Journal of the International Phonetic Association* (2017) and George Shevelov's entry on the Ukrainian language in the *Encyclopedia of Ukraine*. They describe standard Ukrainian. Regional pronunciation varies.

## Before you start

Read [The Ukrainian alphabet](/alphabet/) first. This page assumes you can recognise the letters.

## Vowels

Ukrainian has six vowel sounds, written <Uk>а, о, у, е, и, і</Uk>. Vowel length does not change meaning: there are no pairs of long and short vowels.

The letters <Uk>я, ю, є, ї</Uk> do not add new vowels. They combine one of these vowels with a <Uk>й</Uk> sound or with a soft consonant (see [the alphabet page](/alphabet/)).

### И and І

<Uk>И</Uk> is pronounced further back in the mouth and a little lower than <Uk>і</Uk>. The 2017 study notes that it is closer to the vowel of English "sit" than to a true ee. Mixing them up changes words:

<ExampleList title="И and І">
  <Example uk="дим" en="smoke" />
  <Example uk="дім" en="house, home" />
  <Example uk="кит" en="whale" />
  <Example uk="кіт" en="cat" />
</ExampleList>

### Vowels without stress

In Ukrainian every vowel can appear in a stressed or an unstressed syllable. Shevelov's encyclopedia entry says that in normal speech Ukrainian vowels are **not reduced**. The one exception is <Uk>е</Uk> and <Uk>и</Uk>: without stress the two come close to each other, and the neighbouring sounds decide which one they sound more like. The official orthography says the same: unstressed <Uk>е</Uk> and <Uk>и</Uk> are indistinct, and spelling keeps the letter of the stressed form (§1).

So an unstressed <Uk>о</Uk> is still an o and an unstressed <Uk>а</Uk> is still an a:

<ExampleList title="Unstressed о and а keep their quality">
  <Example uk="доро́га" en="road" note="Both о are pronounced as o; only the middle one is stressed." />
  <Example uk="о́зеро" en="lake" note="Stress on the first о; the last о is still a clear o." />
  <Example uk="зима́" en="winter" />
</ExampleList>

<ExampleList title="Unstressed е and и">
  <Example uk="село́" en="village" note="Here е is unstressed and indistinct; the spelling follows the stressed form." />
  <Example uk="се́ла" en="villages" note="Here е is stressed and clear." />
</ExampleList>

<Note type="info">
  The 2017 study adds a nuance: in fluent, fast speech unstressed vowels can be slightly weaker than stressed ones, and an unstressed <Uk>о</Uk> before a stressed <Uk>у</Uk> may be pronounced a little higher, towards <Uk>у</Uk>. For a learner, the practical advice is the same: pronounce each written vowel fully, and do not swallow unstressed vowels.
</Note>

## Hard and soft consonants

Many Ukrainian consonants come in two versions, **hard** and **soft**. A soft (palatalised) consonant is said with the middle of the tongue raised towards the roof of the mouth, as if a hint of y were blended into it. Both references agree that real hard/soft pairs exist only for the consonants made with the tongue tip just behind the teeth: <Uk>д, т, з, с, ц, дз, л, н, р</Uk>. Other consonants are only slightly softened before <Uk>і</Uk>.

Softness is shown in spelling, not by a separate letter for each soft consonant:

- by <Uk>ь</Uk> at the end of a syllable and before <Uk>о</Uk>;
- by <Uk>я, ю, є</Uk> after a consonant;
- by <Uk>і</Uk> after a consonant.

<ExampleList title="Soft consonants">
  <Example uk="день" en="day" note="Soft н at the end of the word, shown by ь." />
  <Example uk="сіль" en="salt" note="Soft л, shown by ь." />
  <Example uk="сього́дні" en="today" note="Soft с before о, shown by ь." />
  <Example uk="лю́ди" en="people" note="Soft л, shown by ю." />
  <Example uk="дя́дько" en="uncle" note="Soft д shown by я, and another soft д shown by ь." />
</ExampleList>

A hard <Uk>л</Uk> is "dark": the back of the tongue is raised, as in the l at the end of English "full". A soft <Uk>ль</Uk> sounds lighter.

<Note type="remember">
  A soft consonant is one sound, not a consonant followed by a separate y. In <Uk>лю́ди</Uk> you hear a soft <Uk>л</Uk> and then <Uk>у</Uk>, not <Uk>л + й + у</Uk>.
</Note>

## Г and Ґ

<Uk>Г</Uk> is a voiced fricative made in the throat (official orthography, §5). It sounds like an h with the voice switched on. It is **not** the g of "go". The letter for that sound is <Uk>Ґ</Uk>, a stop made with the back of the tongue (official orthography, §6), which is much less common.

<ExampleList title="Г and Ґ">
  <Example uk="гора́" en="mountain" />
  <Example uk="го́ри" en="mountains" />
  <Example uk="ґа́нок" en="porch" />
  <Example uk="ґу́дзик" en="button" />
</ExampleList>

Before <Uk>к</Uk>, the <Uk>г</Uk> sound becomes voiceless, like <Uk>х</Uk>, as in <Uk>легки́й</Uk>:

<Example uk="легки́й" en="light (not heavy); easy" note="The г is pronounced like х here because к follows." />

## В at the end of a syllable

Before a vowel, <Uk>в</Uk> is close to English v. Before rounded vowels it can sound closer to w. At the end of a syllable or a word it is pronounced as a short w-like sound, never as a hard v or f. Both references agree on this.

<ExampleList title="В at the end of a syllable">
  <Example uk="вовк" en="wolf" note="The first в is v-like; the second, at the end of the word, is w-like." />
  <Example uk="любо́в" en="love" />
  <Example uk="за́втра" en="tomorrow" />
  <Example uk="Ки́їв" en="Kyiv" />
</ExampleList>

## Й

<Uk>Й</Uk> is a y sound as in "yes". At the end of a syllable it is a short glide, so <Uk>ай</Uk> is roughly like the vowel in English "high", and <Uk>ій</Uk> roughly like "ee" with a y-glide after it.

<ExampleList title="Й">
  <Example uk="чай" en="tea" />
  <Example uk="мій" en="my (masculine)" />
  <Example uk="моя́" en="my (feminine)" note="After a vowel, я is й + а." />
</ExampleList>

## Щ

<Uk>Щ</Uk> is two sounds, <Uk>ш</Uk> followed by <Uk>ч</Uk>, roughly the "sh-ch" in "fresh cheese". In fast speech it may be shortened.

<ExampleList title="Щ">
  <Example uk="що" en="what; that" />
  <Example uk="ще" en="still; more" />
  <Example uk="борщ" en="borshch (beet soup)" />
  <Example uk="ща́стя" en="happiness" />
</ExampleList>

## The apostrophe

The apostrophe shows that a hard consonant is pronounced separately from a following <Uk>я, ю, є</Uk> or <Uk>ї</Uk>. The vowel letter then has its full <Uk>й</Uk> sound (official orthography, §7). Without the apostrophe, the same letters would mean a soft consonant plus a vowel.

<ExampleList title="The apostrophe in pronunciation">
  <Example uk="м’я́со" en="meat" note="Hard м, then й + а." />
  <Example uk="п’ять" en="five" />
  <Example uk="бур’я́н" en="weeds" note="Hard р, then й + а." />
  <Example uk="буря́к" en="beetroot" note="Soft р, then а. No apostrophe and no й." />
</ExampleList>

## Voicing: what changes and what does not

**No devoicing at the end of a word.** In Ukrainian, voiced consonants stay voiced at the end of a word: <Uk>дуб</Uk> ends in a real <Uk>б</Uk>, and <Uk>ніж</Uk> in a real <Uk>ж</Uk>. The 2017 study points out that this makes Ukrainian different from Russian and most other modern Slavic languages. In fast speech a final voiced consonant may become partly voiceless.

<ExampleList title="Final consonants stay voiced">
  <Example uk="дуб" en="oak" />
  <Example uk="хліб" en="bread" />
  <Example uk="ніж" en="knife" />
</ExampleList>

**Voicing before a voiced consonant.** A voiceless consonant becomes voiced when a voiced consonant follows it. Both references describe this, and Shevelov's entry stresses that it does not work in the opposite direction as a general rule. The spelling does not change.

<ExampleList title="Voicing before a voiced consonant">
  <Example uk="боротьба́" en="struggle, fight" note="The т is pronounced like a soft д because б follows." />
  <Example uk="о́тже" en="so, therefore" note="The т and ж together sound like дж." />
</ExampleList>

**<Uk>П, т, к</Uk> are unaspirated.** Ukrainian <Uk>п, т, к</Uk> are said without a puff of breath after them, and <Uk>б, д, ґ</Uk> are fully voiced. Hold your hand in front of your mouth while you say <Uk>та́то</Uk> (dad): you should feel very little air.

## Common mistakes for English speakers

<Note type="mistake">
  - **Pronouncing <Uk>Г</Uk> as g.** Use an h-like, voiced sound. Keep the g of "go" for <Uk>Ґ</Uk>.
  - **Swallowing unstressed vowels.** Say every written vowel. In <Uk>доро́га</Uk> all three vowels are clearly heard.
  - **Merging <Uk>И</Uk> and <Uk>І</Uk>.** They distinguish words such as <Uk>дим</Uk> (smoke) and <Uk>дім</Uk> (house).
  - **Saying a hard v or f at the end of a syllable.** In <Uk>любо́в</Uk> and <Uk>Ки́їв</Uk> the final <Uk>в</Uk> is w-like.
  - **Adding a separate y to soft consonants.** <Uk>лю́ди</Uk> has a soft <Uk>л</Uk>, not <Uk>л + й</Uk>.
  - **Leaving out the <Uk>й</Uk> after an apostrophe.** <Uk>м’я́со</Uk> has <Uk>м</Uk>, then <Uk>й + а</Uk>.
  - **Devoicing final consonants**, a habit learners can bring from Russian. <Uk>хліб</Uk> ends in <Uk>б</Uk>, not <Uk>п</Uk>.
  - **Reading <Uk>Щ</Uk> as a single sh.** It is <Uk>ш + ч</Uk>.
</Note>

## Limitations of this page

- The English comparisons are rough guides, not exact equivalents. Listening to native speakers is the only reliable way to learn the sounds. This site has no audio.
- Regional pronunciation varies. The references describe the standard language, which is based on the Kyiv area.
- Some details are debated by phoneticians, for example the exact quality of <Uk>и</Uk> and how soft consonants are before <Uk>і</Uk>. The 2017 study says so explicitly.
