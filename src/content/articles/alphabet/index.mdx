## What you will learn

By the end of this page you will be able to name and recognise all 33 letters of the Ukrainian alphabet, you will have a rough idea of the sound each one stands for, and you will know which letters look familiar but mean something different from what an English reader expects.

The sound descriptions here are **approximate**. English and Ukrainian do not share the same set of sounds, so a comparison such as "like the *oo* in *food*" only gets you close. The next page, [Pronunciation basics](/alphabet/pronunciation/), explains the sounds in more detail.

## Before you start

This is the first page of the course, so there is nothing to read first. You do not need to know any other language that uses Cyrillic.

## The alphabet at a glance

Ukrainian is written in a form of the Cyrillic alphabet with 33 letters. Every letter has a capital and a small form (official orthography, section 4.1):

<p lang="uk">А Б В Г Ґ Д Е Є Ж З И І Ї Й К Л М Н О П Р С Т У Ф Х Ц Ч Ш Щ Ь Ю Я</p>

The apostrophe (’) is also used in spelling, but it is not a letter and it is not part of the alphabet (official orthography, section 4.1). It is covered [below](#the-apostrophe).

The letter names below are the official ones from the same alphabet table. Vowels are named by their sound. Most consonants are named with an added <Uk>е</Uk>, so <Uk>б</Uk> is *<Uk>бе</Uk>* and <Uk>л</Uk> is *<Uk>ел</Uk>*.

<Note type="mistake">
  Do not read <Uk>Р</Uk> as p, <Uk>Н</Uk> as h or <Uk>С</Uk> as k. Ukrainian spells p with <Uk>П</Uk>, h-like sounds with <Uk>Г</Uk> or <Uk>Х</Uk>, and k with <Uk>К</Uk>.

## И and І

Ukrainian has two different i-like vowels, and the letters are easy to mix up:

- **<Uk>І і</Uk>** is close to the ee in "see", but short.
- **<Uk>И и</Uk>** is further back in the mouth and a little lower. The phonetic study describes it as further back and lower than <Uk>і</Uk>, close to the IPA vowel [ɪ]. As a rough English comparison (ours, not the study's), it is nearer to the vowel of "sit" than to that of "see".

The difference changes meaning:

<ExampleList title="И and І change the word">
  <Example uk="дім" en="house, home" />
  <Example uk="дим" en="smoke" />
  <Example uk="кіт" en="cat" />
  <Example uk="кит" en="whale" />
</ExampleList>

<Note type="mistake">
  The capital <Uk>И</Uk> looks like a mirrored Latin N, and the small <Uk>и</Uk> looks like a Latin u. Neither stands for n or u. The letter for the u sound is <Uk>у</Uk>.
</Note>

## Г and Ґ

These are two separate letters for two different sounds (official orthography, §5, §6):

- **<Uk>Г г</Uk>** is a fricative made in the throat. It is voiced, so it sounds like a breathy h with the voice switched on. It is the far more common letter.
- **<Uk>Ґ ґ</Uk>** is a stop made with the back of the tongue, like the g in "go". It appears in a limited number of Ukrainian words and in Ukrainian names (official orthography, §6).

  <Example uk="гора́" en="mountain" />
  <Example uk="ґа́нок" en="porch" />
  <Example uk="ґу́дзик" en="button" />
</ExampleList>

## Є, Ї, Ю and Я: one letter, two sounds

The letters **<Uk>є, ю, я</Uk>** stand for two sounds, <Uk>й</Uk> plus a vowel, in three positions: at the start of a word, after a vowel, and after an apostrophe (official orthography, §4). After a consonant they do something different. They show that the consonant is soft and add the vowel <Uk>е, у</Uk> or <Uk>а</Uk> (official orthography, §4).

**<Uk>Ї</Uk>** is simpler: it always stands for <Uk>й</Uk> plus <Uk>і</Uk> (official orthography, §3).

## Й

**<Uk>Й</Uk>** stands for a y sound like the one in "yes". After a vowel it forms a short glide with it, as in <Uk>чай</Uk> (tea) and <Uk>мій</Uk> (my, masculine). At the end of a syllable it is a short i-like glide rather than a full consonant.

## Щ

**<Uk>Щ</Uk>** stands for two sounds together, <Uk>ш</Uk> followed by <Uk>ч</Uk>. English has the same pair across a word boundary in "fresh cheese". In fast speech it can be shortened.

## The soft sign Ь

**<Uk>Ь</Uk>** has no sound of its own. It shows that the consonant before it is soft (palatalised), roughly as if a hint of y were blended into it. It is written after <Uk>д, т, з, с, ц, дз, л, н</Uk> at the end of a syllable (official orthography, §26) and before <Uk>о</Uk> (§8).

<ExampleList title="The soft sign">
  <Example uk="сіль" en="salt" />
  <Example uk="день" en="day" />
  <Example uk="сього́дні" en="today" note="Here ь stands before о: the с is soft." />
</ExampleList>

Some letters you may see in other languages written in Cyrillic, such as Russian, are not part of the Ukrainian alphabet, and Ukrainian has no hard sign (official orthography, section 4.1). If a word contains a letter missing from the table above, it is not Ukrainian spelling.

## The apostrophe

The apostrophe is not a letter, but it matters for pronunciation. It shows that a hard consonant is pronounced separately from the following <Uk>я, ю, є</Uk> or <Uk>ї</Uk>. The vowel then keeps its <Uk>й</Uk> sound (official orthography, §7). Compare:

<ExampleList title="With and without an apostrophe">
  <Example uk="бур’я́н" en="weeds" note="Hard р, then й + а." />
  <Example uk="буря́к" en="beetroot" note="Soft р, then а. No apostrophe." />
  <Example uk="п’ять" en="five" />
  <Example uk="сім’я́" en="family" />
</ExampleList>

The most common places for an apostrophe are after <Uk>б, п, в, м, ф</Uk> and <Uk>р</Uk>, and after a prefix ending in a consonant (official orthography, §7). You do not need these rules to read: when you see ’, pronounce the <Uk>й</Uk>.

<Note type="info">
  This site writes the apostrophe as ’ (a curly apostrophe), as the official orthography does. Many people type the straight keyboard apostrophe instead; it marks the same thing.
</Note>

## Common mistakes

<Note type="mistake">
  - **Reading look-alike letters as Latin.** <Uk>В</Uk> is v, <Uk>Н</Uk> is n, <Uk>Р</Uk> is r, <Uk>С</Uk> is s, <Uk>У</Uk> is u and <Uk>Х</Uk> is a throaty h.
  - **Treating <Uk>Г</Uk> as g.** In Ukrainian, <Uk>Г</Uk> is an h-like sound. The g of "go" is written <Uk>Ґ</Uk>.
  - **Swapping <Uk>И</Uk> and <Uk>І</Uk>.** <Uk>І</Uk> is the ee-like vowel; <Uk>И</Uk> is further back. They distinguish words such as <Uk>дім</Uk> and <Uk>дим</Uk>.
  - **Reading <Uk>Ь</Uk> as a vowel.** It has no sound of its own.
  - **Dropping the <Uk>й</Uk> after an apostrophe.** In <Uk>м’я́со</Uk> you hear <Uk>м</Uk>, then <Uk>й + а</Uk>.
</Note>
